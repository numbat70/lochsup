Breathe Less, Go Faster: How CO2 Tolerance Unlocks Endurance

It is counterintuitive but lack of oxygen does not make you breathless. CO₂ does.

There is usually more oxygen in your lungs and blood stream than you really need BUT as CO₂ levels in your blood stream rise the urge to breathe increases. Most people assume heavy, deep breathing floods the body with oxygen. In reality, arterial blood is already 95–99% saturated with oxygen during normal breathing. Taking larger, faster breaths doesn't significantly increase oxygen uptake; instead, it expels too much Carbon Dioxide (CO₂).

There is something called the the Bohr Effect that says Carbon dioxide isn't just a waste gas—it is the primary catalyst that triggers hemoglobin in your red blood cells to release oxygen into your muscles, heart, and brain. When CO₂ levels drop due to over-breathing, hemoglobin holds onto oxygen tightly, starving your tissues even when your blood is full of it.

CO₂ Tolerance: The urge to breathe is driven primarily by rising CO₂ levels, not a lack of oxygen. People with low CO₂ tolerance feel breathless quickly during exercise, leading to erratic mouth breathing, higher heart rates, and premature fatigue.

The easy way to fight this is to raise your CO₂ tolerance and learn some breathing techniques

Start with working out what your CO₂ tolerance is

There is a standard for this called the BOLT (Body Oxygen Level Test) test.

  1. Sit quietly for a few minutes and breathe normally through your nose.
  2. Take a normal, gentle breath in and a normal breath out through your nose.
  3. Pinch your nose closed and start a timer.

  4. Stop the timer at the very first involuntary urge to breathe, such as a swallow, a subtle twitch in your diaphragm, or the first distinct desire for air. Do not hold until maximum discomfort.

  5. Under 20 seconds: Indicates high CO₂ sensitivity and likely overbreathing during exercise.

  6. 20 to 30 seconds: Represents a functional baseline for everyday activity.
  7. 40+ seconds: Reflects strong CO₂ tolerance and efficient respiratory control.

So how do we improve our CO₂ Tolerance

TLDR: Breathe out and hold your breathe for a bit. Repeat a few time and do this every day.

  • Switch to Nasal Breathing
  • Practice Light Breathing for 5 minutes, with gentle breaths and about 25% less air than usual. You should feel mildly short of breath for the entire time.
  • Implement Cadence breathing. Time your breaths in Steps or Paddle strokes and aim to breath out for twice as long as you breath in.
  • Do all this whilst walking so that you get used to doing it during effort.

This will all help you a bit. The science suggests a bit more structure.

If your BOLT score is 15 Seconds or less do this for 4 weeks

  • Eliminate habitual mouth breathing and building initial CO₂ tolerance. Avoid intense breath holds while running/paddling; keep holds to walking only.
  • Walking Breath Holds: 10–15 paces per hold (repeat 6 times, 45–60 sec rest between holds).
  • Active / Jogging Breath Holds: Skip for Weeks 1–2. In Weeks 3–4,try 5–8 paces during an easy warm-up jog only if recovery takes 2 breaths or fewer.

If your BOLT score is 20 Seconds or more do this for 4 weeks

  • Move from resting nasal efficiency into active Zone 2 endurance efforts.
  • Walking Breath Holds: 15–25 paces per hold (repeat 6–8 times, 45 sec rest).
  • Active / Jogging Breath Holds: 8–12 paces per hold at an easy aerobic pace (repeat 5–6 times, 60 sec rest).

If your BOLT score is 40 Seconds or more do this for 4 weeks

  • Create strong hypoxic and hypercapnic stress to boost red blood cell release and buffer lactate.
  • Walking Breath Holds: 25–40+ paces per hold (repeat 6–8 times, 30–45 sec rest).
  • Active / Jogging Breath Holds: 12–20 paces per hold at a steady aerobic pace (repeat 6–8 times, 45–60 sec rest).
